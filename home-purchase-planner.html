Checklists

Red flags

  • You are pushed to pay a token amount today.
  • The price is well below neighbouring flats with no clear reason.
  • "The occupancy certificate has been applied for and will come soon."
  • Part of the payment is asked for in cash.
  • The seller or builder says you don't need your own lawyer, or won't show original approvals.
  • The possession date in the advertisement differs from the date on the RERA site.
  • Guaranteed rent or assured return schemes.
  • A metro line or road that is "coming soon" is used to justify the price.
  • The carpet area in the brochure differs from the agreement.
  • Many flats in the same tower are on sale at or below your price.

Questions to ask residents

  • Where does the water come from, and how often do tankers come in summer?
  • How long are the power cuts, and what does backup cover?
  • What is the real monthly maintenance, and has it gone up recently?
  • Has the builder handed over to the owners' association? Any disputes?
  • How long does your commute take at 9 am and 6 pm?
  • What did flats here actually sell or rent for in the last six months?
  • What do you wish you had known before buying?

Guide

Short notes on how to think about the decision. Read once, then come back when a term is unclear.

Ten rules before you buy
  1. Buy a home to live in when you expect to stay seven years or more. Under five years, buying and selling costs usually eat the gain.
  2. Fix your budget from your salary first, then look at flats. Never the other way round.
  3. Keep the home EMI at or under 30–35% of take-home pay, and all EMIs together under 40%.
  4. Keep the flat price within about four to five times your yearly take-home pay.
  5. Hold an emergency fund of six months of expenses that the purchase does not touch. Make it nine to twelve months with one income or irregular income.
  6. Bring 20–25% of the price yourself, plus roughly 8–10% more for stamp duty, registration and other costs.
  7. Location beats amenities: a short commute, transport that already runs, water, schools and hospitals.
  8. Pay for what exists today. A planned metro line or road is a bonus, not a reason.
  9. Have the papers checked by your own lawyer, not the seller's or the builder's.
  10. If the numbers only work when prices rise fast, the numbers don't work.
One income, two incomes, a family

One income, no dependents. You can take a little more risk, but you are your only safety net. EMI up to about 35% of take-home, six months of expenses aside, and health cover of your own, separate from your employer's.

One income with dependents. This is the most fragile setup. Hold the EMI near 30%, keep nine months of expenses, and take term life cover at least equal to the loan before you sign.

Two incomes. The bank will lend against both. Plan so that the larger income alone could carry the EMI for a year, because careers pause for children, health, layoffs or study. Decide ownership shares and who claims which tax benefit before registration.

Self-employed or variable pay. Use your lowest reliable monthly income, not the average. Add three months to the emergency fund. Lenders usually ask for two to three years of tax returns.

Supporting parents or planning children. Put those costs into the salary step now, even if they start later. A budget that only works for today's life will not survive the loan's twenty years.

Buying to live in or to invest

To live in. Judge it by commute, comfort, stability and whether the EMI lets you sleep. A home you live in is partly an expense, like rent, and partly an asset.

To invest. Judge it like any investment: net rent return, likely price growth, how easily you can sell, and what the same money earns elsewhere. Rent return on Indian city flats is often only 2–4% a year, usually below the loan interest rate, so most of the return has to come from price growth.

Leverage cuts both ways. A loan magnifies gains when prices rise faster than the interest rate, and magnifies losses when they don't. You still owe the EMI when the flat is empty.

Selling is slow and costly. A flat can take months to sell, and you cannot sell one bedroom when you need money. Count that before putting most of your savings into one flat.

Ready or under construction, builder or resale

Ready with an occupancy certificate. You see exactly what you get, you stop paying rent as soon as you move, and there is no GST. The price is usually a little higher.

Under construction. Lower entry price and staged payments, but there is delay risk, GST applies, and you may pay rent and EMI together for years. Check the completion date and quarterly progress on the RERA site, not in the brochure.

From a builder. Check approvals, the builder's delivery record on earlier projects, and what the agreement says about delay.

Resale. Check the seller's title, any loan on the flat, unpaid dues, and that original documents exist. Expect a broker fee. The advantage is that you can see the real building, the real neighbours and the real maintenance.

Carpet, built-up and super built-up area

Carpet area is the usable floor inside the flat, including internal walls, and excluding outer walls, shafts, balconies and open terraces. This is the figure RERA requires builders to state.

Built-up area adds the outer walls and balconies. Super built-up area adds your share of lobbies, lifts, stairs and the clubhouse.

Loading is how much larger the super built-up area is than the carpet area. Flats are quoted per super built-up square foot, so compare options on price per carpet square foot. Carpet below about 65% of super built-up means you are paying heavily for common areas.

How a home loan really works

Floating rates. Most bank home loans are linked to the RBI repo rate plus the bank's spread. When the repo rate moves, your rate follows at the next reset. The spread stays for the life of the loan, so negotiate it at the start.

Early years are mostly interest. In the first years, most of each EMI goes to interest. A prepayment made early saves far more than the same amount later.

Tenure. A longer tenure lowers the EMI but raises total interest. A sensible approach is to take a comfortable EMI and prepay every year from bonuses and increments.

Prepayment. Banks cannot charge individuals a penalty for prepaying a floating-rate home loan.

How much they lend. RBI limits loans to about 90% of the property value up to ₹30 lakh, 80% from ₹30 to ₹75 lakh, and 75% above ₹75 lakh. Stamp duty and registration are generally not financed.

Insurance. Cover the loan with a plain term life policy. You are not required to buy the lender's own insurance product. If told otherwise, ask for it in writing.

Review yearly. If new borrowers get a much lower rate than you, ask your lender to reprice, or move the loan.

Where to keep the down payment while you save

Money you need within about three years should not depend on the share market. A fall just before you buy can set you back years.

Keep it where the value is stable and you can withdraw on time, such as bank deposits or other low-risk, short-term options. Accept the lower return. The job of this money is to be there on the day.

Keep the emergency fund separate from the down payment, in a different account if that helps you leave it alone.

Other ways to invest, in brief

This is general education, not a recommendation. Each has its own risks.

  • Bank deposits and government small savings. Stable value, modest return, some have lock-ins.
  • Provident funds (EPF, PPF). Long lock-ins, tax advantages, suited to retirement money.
  • Debt mutual funds. Usually steadier than shares, but not guaranteed.
  • Equity index funds. Higher long-run growth potential with sharp ups and downs. Suited to money you won't need for seven years or more.
  • REITs. Listed units that hold rented commercial property. A small-ticket way to own property income, with prices that move daily.
  • Gold. A diversifier, with no income.

Against these, a second flat means a large single asset, a loan, low rent return and slow resale. It can still work, but only at the right price.

Negotiating the price
  • Find out three recent deal prices in the same building from residents and from brokers on both sides. Portal prices are asking prices.
  • Count how many similar flats are on sale. Many sellers means you can bargain.
  • Negotiate the total: parking, club fees, maintenance advance and fittings, not only the rate per square foot.
  • A clean bank payment and a quick closing are worth a discount to most sellers.
  • Be ready to walk away. There is always another flat.
Glossary
  • EMI. The fixed monthly loan payment, part interest and part principal.
  • LTV. Loan to value: the loan as a share of the property value.
  • Repo rate. The RBI's policy rate that most home loan rates follow.
  • Spread. What the bank adds on top of the repo rate.
  • FOIR. All your EMIs as a share of income. Lenders use it to decide how much to lend.
  • RERA. The state real estate regulator where projects must be registered.
  • OC. Occupancy certificate: the authority's confirmation that the building is fit to live in and matches the approved plan.
  • CC. Commencement certificate: permission to start construction.
  • EC. Encumbrance certificate: the record of registered sales and mortgages on the property.
  • Khata. The municipal record of the property and its owner, used for property tax.
  • Mother deed. The earliest document in the chain of ownership.
  • Sale agreement and sale deed. The agreement sets the terms. The registered sale deed transfers ownership.
  • Guidance value. The government's minimum value for registration. It is a floor, not the market price.
  • Pre-EMI. Interest-only payments while a flat is under construction.
  • TDS on property. Tax the buyer deducts from the seller's payment on higher-value purchases.
